Point-Mass EV Lap Simulation

MATLAB · Vehicle dynamics · Battery modeling · Accumulator sizing

MATLAB point-mass vehicle simulation developed to predict Formula SAE EV performance and support accumulator sizing. The model couples track geometry and longitudinal vehicle dynamics with motor, electrical, battery thermal, and cooling models to estimate lap time, energy consumption, pack voltage and current, state of charge, and battery temperature.

Overview

I developed the simulation for Bruin Formula Racing to evaluate vehicle performance and help size the accumulator for our Formula SAE EV. Pack configuration affects more than energy capacity: changing the number of series and parallel cells changes pack voltage, current capability, voltage sag, thermal loading, mass, and ultimately the performance available from the vehicle.

Rather than evaluating these effects independently, the model couples vehicle dynamics and accumulator behavior throughout an endurance event. At each distance step, the simulation determines the vehicle state and requested power, calculates the corresponding electrical load, and updates pack voltage, state of charge, and battery temperature.

I used the resulting model to compare candidate accumulator configurations and support selection of a 114s6p pack for the 2026–27 vehicle.

Model outputs

  • Lap and endurance time
  • Vehicle speed and acceleration
  • Motor speed, torque, and power
  • Accumulator current and voltage
  • Energy consumption
  • State of charge
  • Battery temperature
  • Regenerative energy
  • Current-limited available torque

Point-mass vehicle model

The vehicle is represented as a point mass and propagated around the track in small distance steps. Track geometry is loaded from a CSV containing segment type, radius, and distance, allowing the simulation to apply different calculations to straights, corner entry, braking zones, and steady-state corners.

On straights, acceleration is limited by available motor torque and power, vehicle drag, top speed, and the electrical capability of the accumulator. During corner entry, available longitudinal force is reduced by the lateral acceleration required to follow the corner. Braking zones are solved backward from the required corner speed so the model can determine where the vehicle must begin decelerating.

The motor model uses performance data for the EMRAX 228 to determine available torque and power as a function of motor speed. Motor and drivetrain losses are also included when converting between mechanical and electrical power.

Ftractive = Twheel / rwheel

Fnet = Ftractive − Fresistive

a = Fnet / m

Accumulator electrical model

The vehicle simulation is coupled to an electrical model of the accumulator so battery limitations can directly affect vehicle performance. Mechanical power demand from the vehicle model is converted into electrical power and pack current at each simulation step.

Cell discharge data is used to relate state of charge to open-circuit voltage. Internal resistance is then used to estimate voltage sag under load. As energy is removed from the pack, the model updates state of charge and the corresponding cell voltage before calculating the next simulation step.

This coupling allows the model to account for the fact that available vehicle power is not constant throughout an endurance event. If requested power would require more current than the selected accumulator configuration can provide, the model limits the available motor torque.

Model validation

I compared simulated accumulator state of charge against BMS telemetry from the vehicle to evaluate whether the electrical model reproduced measured energy usage. The simulated SOC follows the measured trend over the validation interval, while the remaining divergence provides a way to identify assumptions and parameters that can be improved.

Comparing simulation results with vehicle telemetry provides a check on the combined vehicle and electrical model using measured data rather than evaluating the model only from its own predicted outputs.

Battery thermal model

The simulation includes a lumped thermal model of the accumulator to estimate battery temperature throughout the endurance event. Joule heating is calculated at each time step from battery current and internal resistance, while heat transfer to the environment is modeled using ambient temperature and conductive/convective and radiative heat-loss terms.

Qgen = I²R

Cth dT/dt = Qgen − Qloss

Qloss = hc(Tbatt − Tamb) + hr(Tbatt4 − Tamb4)

The accumulator is treated as a lumped thermal mass with a single temperature state and thermal capacitance. The battery temperature is updated at each simulation step as current and power demand change around the track.

Temperature is also coupled back into the electrical model. The simulated battery temperature is used in temperature-dependent state-of-charge calculations, allowing thermal conditions to influence the estimated usable capacity as the endurance event progresses.

This is particularly useful when comparing accumulator configurations. Reducing parallel-cell count decreases accumulator mass, but increases current per cell, Joule heating, voltage sag, and temperature rise. The thermal model therefore allows pack mass, electrical performance, energy capacity, and temperature to be evaluated together.

Cooling and auxiliary loads

The simulation also accounts for power that does not directly produce tractive force. Motor and inverter losses contribute to the vehicle's thermal load, while the cooling system and DC-DC loads contribute to total electrical energy consumption.

Cooling behavior is modeled dynamically rather than as a single constant power draw, allowing fan and pump operation to change during the simulated event. Including these auxiliary loads gives a more complete estimate of the energy that must be supplied by the accumulator over endurance.

Accumulator sizing

One of the primary uses of the simulation was comparing accumulator configurations for the 2026–27 vehicle. Changing series count affects operating voltage and motor performance, while changing parallel count affects capacity, current per cell, voltage sag, temperature, and pack mass.

Instead of selecting the accumulator from energy capacity alone, candidate configurations were run through the complete vehicle simulation. This allowed each configuration to be evaluated based on endurance energy margin, electrical limits, thermal behavior, and the performance effect of accumulator mass.

The analysis supported selection of a 114s6p accumulator using 684 cells, with a maximum voltage of 478.8 V and approximately 7.35 kWh of nominal energy.

Endurance simulation

A representative simulation of the selected 114s6p configuration begins at 95% state of charge and models the complete endurance event while continuously updating the vehicle and accumulator states.

In one simulated endurance run, the vehicle used approximately 5.53 kWh and finished at 26.6% state of charge, with a simulated lap time of approximately 70.8 seconds. These results provide an estimate of remaining energy margin while simultaneously checking electrical and thermal behavior throughout the event.

Model development

The simulation has been developed iteratively as additional vehicle, motor, cell, and telemetry data became available. Early versions focused primarily on the vehicle speed profile and energy demand; the model was then expanded to couple electrical limitations, state of charge, battery temperature, and cooling behavior back into the vehicle simulation.

Validation against measured vehicle data is being used to identify where the model can be improved. The goal is not only to predict a lap time, but to build a tool that can support vehicle-level design decisions such as accumulator sizing and powertrain limits.
