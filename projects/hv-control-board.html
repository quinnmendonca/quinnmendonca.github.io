HV Control Board (Ultimate HV Board)

2026–27 Formula SAE EV

I designed this board to handle the accumulator's high-voltage control and safety functions at up to 478.8 V: precharge, solid-state discharge, AIR control, HV-present and AIR weld detection, the TSMPs, and the HV indicator and ready-to-move lights. It sits inside the accumulator in a section separated from the cells, as one of three boards in the electronics stack with the BMS Parent and HV Isolated Backplane.

Overview

The HV Control Board interfaces the low-voltage BMS and shutdown system with circuitry connected to the tractive system. Instead of putting separate HV control boards around the vehicle, I consolidated the main accumulator control, monitoring, and safety circuits onto one board inside the pack.

The board controls the positive and negative accumulator isolation relays and the precharge relay, actively discharges the vehicle-side DC link, checks whether HV is present, detects welded AIRs, and contains the tractive-system measurement-point circuitry. It also handles the logic and drivers for the HV indicator and ready-to-move lights.

Keeping these functions inside the accumulator reduces the amount of HV electronics outside the pack and simplifies the wiring between the accumulator, service section, and vehicle.

Main functions

  • Precharge control
  • Solid-state active discharge
  • HV-present detection
  • AIR weld detection
  • AIR and precharge relay control
  • Tractive-system measurement-point circuitry
  • HV indicator-light control
  • Ready-to-move (RTM) light control

Design requirements

The board was designed around our 114s6p accumulator, with a maximum tractive-system voltage of 478.8 V. The main constraints were meeting Formula SAE precharge and discharge timing requirements, keeping the high- and low-voltage domains separated, and fitting the electronics inside the accumulator.

The precharge system has to charge the inverter DC link to at least 90% of accumulator voltage within 5 seconds. After shutdown, the discharge circuit has to bring the tractive system below 60 V within 5 seconds.

  • Maximum pack voltage: 478.8 V
  • Precharge: ≥90% of accumulator voltage within 5 s
  • Discharge: <60 V within 5 s
  • Four-layer PCB with 5 mm HV/LV separation
  • Packaged inside the accumulator in a three-PCB electronics stack

Precharge

Before the main AIRs close, the inverter's 650 µF DC link has to be charged without a huge inrush current. A 2.2 kΩ resistor sits in series with the precharge relay and limits the charging current. Once precharge is complete and the main AIR closes, the resistor and precharge relay are bypassed.

The BMS drives the precharge relay through a low-side NMOS with a gate resistor and a pull-down so the gate never floats. A TVS across the coil clamps the flyback when the relay turns off. Every relay driver on the board also has a debug LED so I can see the commanded state without connecting a meter.

Component sizing

With a 650 µF inverter DC-link capacitance and a maximum pack voltage of 478.8 V, the 2.2 kΩ resistor gets the DC link to 90% of pack voltage in approximately 3.29 s, which fits within the 5 s requirement. It sees 104.2 W at the start of precharge and absorbs 74.5 J over the event. I also checked the resistor against its pulse-energy rating.

Discharge

After shutdown, a 2.7 kΩ resistor is switched across the HV bus to drain the energy stored on the inverter DC link. The solid-state switch uses two 1000 V depletion-mode NMOSs connected source-to-source and driven by an FDA217 photovoltaic isolator.

I chose depletion-mode MOSFETs because they conduct at zero gate-source voltage. If the 12 V shutdown supply is lost, the MOSFETs return to their conducting state and the discharge path turns on by default. During normal operation, the FDA217 generates a negative gate-source voltage to hold the MOSFETs off while maintaining galvanic isolation between the low-voltage shutdown system and the tractive system.

The FDA217 input is driven at approximately 5 mA. I also included optional footprints for gate-source resistance, capacitance, and clamping so I can adjust the gate network during testing without another board revision.

Switching simulation

I used LTspice to check the switching behavior at the maximum 478.8 V accumulator voltage. The 2.7 kΩ resistor in the simulation represents the actual discharge resistor in series with the solid-state switch.

With zero gate-source bias, the normally-on depletion-mode MOSFETs conduct and approximately 177 mA flows through the discharge resistor. When the FDA217 applies approximately −12.5 V between each gate and its source, the MOSFETs turn off and the simulated discharge current falls to zero.

Development and testing

The discharge circuit went through several revisions during development. An earlier solid-state implementation switched correctly during low-voltage bench testing, but increasing the test voltage revealed excessive off-state leakage and heating.

That changed how I test the circuit. Instead of only checking whether the switch turns on and off, I also measure off-state current and gate-source voltage as the bus voltage is increased. The optional gate resistance, capacitance, and clamping footprints on this revision give me places to adjust the gate network during testing if needed.

The current revision is undergoing stepped-voltage validation before full-voltage testing.

Power resistor & thermal design

The precharge, discharge, and TSMP circuits all use high-power resistors, but they see very different loads. Precharge and discharge handle short energy pulses as the inverter DC link is charged or discharged, while the TSMP resistors can see full pack voltage continuously. I sized each resistor and heatsink for its worst-case operating condition.

Precharge

Precharge uses a single 2.2 kΩ, 150 W Vishay LTO150H resistor. With a 478.8 V accumulator and 650 µF DC-link capacitance, the circuit reaches 90% of pack voltage in approximately 3.29 s. The resistor initially dissipates 104.2 W and absorbs approximately 74.5 J during the event.

R = 2.2 kΩ

τ = RC = 2.2 kΩ × 650 µF = 1.43 s

t90 = τ ln(10) = 3.29 s

Ipeak = 0.218 A

Ppeak = 104.2 W

Eresistor = ½CV² = 74.5 J

Pavg = 22.6 W

θSA ≤ 5.26 °C/W

The thermal analysis gave a required heatsink thermal resistance of 5.26 °C/W or lower. I paired the resistor with an Ohmite FA-T220-38E heatsink using thermal interface material and clip mounting to provide the thermal path from the resistor case.

Discharge

Active discharge uses a single 2.7 kΩ, 100 W Riedon PF2472 resistor. Starting from the maximum 478.8 V DC-link voltage, the calculated time to fall below 60 V is approximately 3.65 s, within the 5 s requirement. After 5 s, the calculated DC-link voltage is approximately 27.8 V.

R = 2.7 kΩ

τ = RC = 2.7 kΩ × 650 µF = 1.755 s

t60V = 3.65 s

V(5 s) = 27.8 V

Ipeak = 0.177 A

Ppeak = 84.9 W

Eresistor = 74.5 J

Pavg = 20.4 W

θSA ≤ 5.6 °C/W

That gives a required heatsink thermal resistance of 5.6 °C/W or lower. The discharge resistor is paired with an Ohmite FA-T220-25E heatsink.

TSMP resistors

Each tractive-system measurement-point line uses a 15 kΩ, 100 W Vishay LTO100 resistor. Unlike the precharge and discharge resistors, these have to be sized for continuous operation. If the two measurement points are shorted together at maximum pack voltage, 15.96 mA flows through the two resistors in series and each resistor dissipates 3.82 W continuously.

R = 15 kΩ per TSMP line

Ishort = 478.8 V / 30 kΩ = 15.96 mA

Presistor = 3.82 W

Ptotal = 7.64 W

θSA ≤ 13.2 °C/W

This gives a required heatsink thermal resistance of 13.2 °C/W or lower. Each TSMP resistor is mounted to an Ohmite WA-T247-101E heatsink.

HV-present detection

I wanted the 60 V check to be pure hardware so the safety indication doesn't depend on software. Five 1 MΩ resistors on the high side and 120 kΩ + 10 kΩ on the low side scale the HV bus voltage for an LM393 comparator. Splitting the high side across five resistors also keeps the voltage across each resistor to about 93 V at full pack voltage.

The comparator reference is 1.412 V, giving a calculated switching threshold of approximately 55.7 V, slightly below the 60 V threshold. Above that point, the comparator output turns on a PMOS stage that drives an optocoupler across the HV/LV isolation boundary.

The resulting hardware signal is used by the vehicle's safety-light circuitry. Setting the threshold in hardware means the HV-present indication does not depend on a software voltage measurement.

Ready-to-move light

I made the ready-to-move flasher a hardware 555 oscillator so the BMS doesn't have to continuously generate the waveform. With R52 = 270 kΩ and C12 = 1 µF, the design gives an approximately 2.7 Hz flashing frequency.

The timer output drives an NMOS/PMOS stage that switches the external 12 V RTM light. Keeping the oscillator in hardware gives the light its flashing behavior without requiring the BMS to generate it in software.

AIR weld detection

This circuit checks whether an AIR is still closed after it was told to open. Two isolated solid-state switches connect the HV sensing path only when a measurement is needed. A high-impedance divider then scales the measured voltage for an LM393 comparator referenced to 1.606 V.

If the AIR opened properly, the divider reads 0 V. If it's welded, the divider still sees pack voltage. At the 285 V undervoltage lockout, the divider output is 1.92 V, while the comparator trips at about 238 V, giving roughly 20% margin.

The comparator drives a PMOS and optocoupler so the BMS gets an isolated digital weld-status signal. The isolated switches also let me disconnect the sensing divider when I don't need a measurement instead of leaving the measurement path connected all the time.

What I changed after the first board

On the previous revision, the isolated output could spike above what the BMS GPIO can tolerate. I added a 3.3 V zener to clamp the output, while a 33 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider sets the logic level.

Building and testing that circuit separately also let me debug the sensing divider, comparator threshold, isolation stage, and output before integrating the whole function onto this board.

PCB layout

It's a four-layer board containing both low-voltage control electronics and circuits connected to the 478.8 V tractive system. I kept the LV control circuitry on one side of the board and the HV circuitry on the other, with a 5 mm keepout across the isolation boundary. At the isolator packages themselves, the spacing is 4.7 mm.

There is no LV ground plane underneath the isolated HV circuitry. On the LV side, I use ground pours with stitching vias to tie the planes together and give signals short return paths. Anything that crosses the HV/LV boundary does so through its intended isolation component.

The precharge, discharge, and TSMP power resistors also have heatsink mounting provisions built into the PCB. Connector placement and the board outline are constrained by the accumulator enclosure and the three-PCB electronics stack, so the layout had to work around the mechanical packaging as well as the electrical isolation requirements.
