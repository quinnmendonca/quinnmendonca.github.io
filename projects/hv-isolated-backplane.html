HV Isolated Backplane PCB

2026–27 Formula SAE EV · Accumulator electronics integration

I designed this board as the low-voltage backplane at the base of the accumulator's three-board electronics stack. The BMS Parent and HV Control Board plug into it, and the backplane connects them to the vehicle, service section, and sensors. Although it interconnects boards with circuitry connected to the tractive system, the backplane itself stays entirely in the low-voltage domain.

Overview

The main reason for this board was to replace a large amount of point-to-point wiring inside the accumulator with one defined electrical interface. Instead of separately wiring the BMS, HV Control Board, service-section components, sensors, and vehicle harness together, those connections are collected on the backplane.

I drew the top-level schematic around the whole accumulator before placing any connectors. I started with every signal that had to move between the BMS, HV Control Board, vehicle, and service section, then used that to define the connectors and pinout.

This also gives us one place to define system-level net names and connector pinouts. If I need to trace an AIR command, CAN line, sensor signal, or fault signal during debugging, I can follow it through the backplane instead of tracing it through a bundle of individual wires.

Main functions

  • 12 V low-voltage power distribution
  • Shutdown-circuit power distribution
  • CAN communication routing
  • AIR and precharge control routing
  • AIR weld-detection signal routing
  • HVIL and IMD signal routing
  • Current-sensor and E-meter interfaces
  • Temperature-sensor interfaces
  • Charging and J1772 signal routing
  • Debug and safety-light connections

Interconnect architecture

There are four main interfaces: a vehicle-side low-voltage connector with 26 connected pins, the BMS Parent connector, the HV Control Board connector, and a connector to the service section. The service-section connection reaches the AIRs, current sensor, IMD, E-meter, and six temperature sensors inside the accumulator.

Most of the control traffic runs between the BMS Parent and HV Control Board. The BMS sends the positive AIR, negative AIR, and precharge commands through the backplane, along with the two enables used by the AIR weld-detection circuit. The weld result is then routed back to the BMS.

CAN, fan PWM, BMS fault, and the J1772 charging signals run between the vehicle connector and the BMS Parent. Other signals only need to move between the two boards in the stack, so they never have to leave the accumulator through the external harness.

I tried to keep that distinction clear in the schematic: signals that only need to move between boards stay internal to the stack, while vehicle and service-section signals leave through their respective connectors. That keeps the external harness from carrying signals that don't actually need to leave the accumulator electronics.

Debug and safety interfaces

I brought the positive AIR, negative AIR, and precharge command lines out to debug pins on the vehicle connector. That means I can watch what the BMS is actually commanding from outside the accumulator instead of opening the enclosure and probing the board stack directly.

This is useful when a problem could be either a BMS command issue or something on the HV Control Board. I can check whether the command reached the backplane first and narrow down which side of the system I need to debug.

The HVIL loop and IMD fault line are different. They are straight pass-throughs between the vehicle connector and service section, and the backplane doesn't make decisions based on them. It just provides the connection through the accumulator electronics assembly.

Power management

The accumulator electronics can run from one of two 12 V sources: ready power when the car is running and charge power when the car is charging. Both need to power the same LV rail, but I don't want either source feeding backwards into the other one.

Each input first goes through a fuse. A 1N4007 is connected from the protected input to ground so that if the supply polarity is reversed, current is directed through the diode and the fuse opens instead of applying the reversed supply to the electronics.

After that, each source goes through a B340 Schottky diode onto the common LV power rail. The diodes OR the ready and charge supplies together while preventing one source from back-feeding the other. A 20 kΩ resistor holds the rail at ground when neither source is present.

Shutdown power

Shutdown power (SD PWR) is kept separate from the normal LV rail. It goes directly through the backplane to the BMS Parent and HV Control Board and powers the parts of the system that have to respond directly to the shutdown circuit.

On the HV Control Board, that includes the AIR drivers, their debug circuitry, and the FDA217 photovoltaic driver for the solid-state discharge switch. Keeping this supply separate lets those circuits respond directly when the shutdown circuit opens instead of depending on the rest of the LV electronics turning off.

This is also part of the fail-safe behavior of the discharge circuit. During normal operation, SD PWR lets the FDA217 apply negative gate-source bias to hold the normally-on depletion MOSFETs off. If shutdown power disappears, that bias disappears and the discharge switch returns to its conducting state.

Service-section and sensor interfaces

The service-section connector handles the signals that need to leave the electronics stack but stay inside the accumulator. This includes connections to the AIRs, current sensor, IMD, E-meter, and temperature sensors.

The current sensor and E-meter both need to communicate with the rest of the vehicle electronics, so the backplane provides their connection into the accumulator's LV system and CAN network. Keeping those connections on the backplane also avoids running separate harnesses directly to the BMS board.

E-meter temperature sensing

The E-meter interface carries low-voltage power, CAN, and a one-wire temperature signal. The temperature sensor is physically away from the electronics stack, so that signal enters the backplane from an external wire before reaching the rest of the electronics.

I added an ESD protection stage on that line at the backplane interface. That gives the external sensor connection a defined protection point before the signal continues into the electronics stack.

HV isolation architecture

The "HV isolated" part of the name doesn't mean the backplane itself contains the HV isolation circuitry. The isolation happens on the functional boards before any HV-referenced signal reaches the backplane.

For example, the HV-present and AIR weld circuits are connected to the tractive system on the HV Control Board. Both cross the HV/LV boundary through optocouplers there. By the time those signals reach the backplane, they are already low-voltage logic signals and can be routed alongside the other control signals.

That lets the backplane interconnect boards that contain 478.8 V circuitry without routing tractive-system voltage through the backplane itself. HV/LV separation is handled locally on the boards that contain both voltage domains, while the shared backplane remains entirely low-voltage.

Design approach

This board is more of a system-integration problem than a complicated analog circuit. The hard part is keeping track of a lot of power, communication, control, safety, sensor, and debug connections while making the interfaces between each part of the accumulator obvious.

I designed it from the system level down. I started with what each subsystem needed to send or receive, grouped those signals by destination and function, and then built the connector pinouts around that. That was easier to reason about than starting with connectors and trying to route everything afterward.

It also means the BMS Parent and HV Control Board have defined interfaces instead of depending on a particular internal harness. If one of those boards changes in a future revision, the backplane schematic gives us one place to see exactly what the rest of the accumulator expects from it.

The final result is a much cleaner connection between the BMS, HV Control Board, service-section electronics, sensors, and vehicle harness while keeping the shared backplane entirely on the low-voltage side of the accumulator.
